H.R.859 · Suspension and passage

Telling buyers when a connected device has a camera or microphone

What would change

This bill would require manufacturers of covered internet-connected devices to clearly tell buyers before purchase whether a camera or microphone is built in. The Federal Trade Commission would enforce that disclosure rule.

Phones, laptops, tablets and other devices people would reasonably expect to have a camera or microphone are excluded.

Full meaning and material qualifications

H.R.859 would require the manufacturer of a covered device to disclose clearly and conspicuously, before purchase, whether the device contains a camera or microphone as a component. Its covered-device definition uses the printed consumer-product reference to section 3(a) of the Consumer Product Safety Act and requires internet capability and a camera or microphone. It excludes telephones including mobile phones, laptops, tablets and devices a consumer would reasonably expect to have a camera or microphone; devices specifically marketed as a camera, telecommunications device or microphone; and devices or apparatus described in the named Communications Act provisions (47 U.S.C. 255, 617, 619 and 303(aa)/(bb)) and associated regulations. These are the bill's categories and literal incorporations, not classifications of actual products.

A section 2 violation would receive the bill's specified unfair-or-deceptive-rule treatment. The FTC would enforce the Act through the incorporated applicable FTC Act terms, with the stated penalties, privileges and immunities and a savings clause for its other authority. No particular penalty amount or additional unstated enforcement power is asserted.

Within 180 days after enactment, the FTC would issue guidance through outreach to relevant private entities. The guidance would address clear, conspicuous and age-appropriate disclosure as practicable, and best practices for pictorial representation. The referenced definition uses includes for pictures, photographs, video, illustrations and symbols; the bill does not independently require a particular icon. Manufacturers could petition for tailored guidance consistent with existing or successor rules of practice. Guidance would not confer rights on any person, State or locality, or bind the FTC or any person. An enforcement action must allege a specific statutory violation; inconsistency with guidance alone is insufficient unless the practices allegedly violate section 2.

The bill would apply to covered devices manufactured after the date 180 days after the FTC issues section 3(c) guidance. It would not apply to devices manufactured or sold before that date, or otherwise introduced into interstate commerce before it. The enactment-relative guidance deadline and the guidance-relative applicability date are distinct; no clock is found to have started from House passage.

The five dated statutory context readings explain the pictorial reference and named accessibility frameworks while preserving their printed conditions and reference notes. They do not add those baseline accessibility duties to H.R.859. Incorporated regulations and category definitions remain literal references; this candidate does not determine exhaustive current regulatory coverage, classify an actual device or certify current law.

The bill excludes specified ordinary recording devices and named Communications Act categories. FTC guidance would be nonbinding. The guidance deadline is 180 days after enactment; the applicability date is 180 days after guidance is issued. Earlier manufacture, sale or introduction into interstate commerce is excluded. House passage does not establish enactment or start either clock.

Hunt Part A amendment 7 to H.R.8800 · Amendment

Overriding some California restrictions on an oil pipeline

What would change

This amendment would let the operator run the Santa Ynez pipeline without California permits or approvals. The Defense Department could choose to acquire permanent rights to use the route, including through compulsory acquisition with compensation even without an owner’s agreement. These would be rights to use the land, not ownership of the land itself. The stated aim is to move offshore oil to refineries supplying military facilities in California.

Federal pipeline safety rules and compensation requirements would still apply. This vote did not make the proposal law or establish that land-use rights were acquired or oil was flowing.

Explanation limits and technical detail

From offshore platforms Harmony, Heritage and Hondo, through Las Flores Canyon and approximately four miles of Gaviota State Park, to Pentland Station in Kern County.

Defense could acquire permanent rights to use the route, including through condemnation with compensation; it would not acquire ownership of all the land. Acquisition is discretionary. Upon acquisition, Defense must license the operator's use. Immediate possession requires both a declaration of taking and an estimated compensation deposit. The license has narrow revocation exceptions and requires applicable federal pipeline safety compliance.

Acquisition is discretionary; license issuance is required only after acquisition. Offshore crude transport to domestic refineries supplying California Defense installations is the authorized purpose, not authenticated actual supply or contracts. The operative text identifies the operator of the Santa Ynez Pipeline System, not a company name.

Section (d)(2)(A) permits the specified license action by an Act of Congress expressly referencing the section, or by the Defense Secretary following the specified final federal safety order (or sustaining appellate judgment), an imminent public-safety hazard, and failure to cure after notice and opportunity. The state-court rule in (d)(2)(B) is distinct.

Military departments already had certain land-acquisition and condemnation powers. The change provides specific Santa Ynez authority, operator licensing and state-law protections; it is not the first federal condemnation power. The exact 21-day baseline detail remains a separate unaccepted fact-map supplement and is not used here.

The operative text does not name the company, refinery or military installation. This draft does not establish actual oil flow, a restart, removal of a particular current court order, a supply shortage, price changes or safety outcomes.

The amendment prints subchapter III of 40 USC chapter 31, while the retained dated Code places the relevant acquisition provisions in II. Preserve without correction or a legal-invalidity conclusion.

  • (e)(2) No California or California-agency authorization, lease, permit or approval is required as a condition of operation, maintenance, repair or use of any pipeline segment. This branch has no halt/substantial-delay condition.
  • (d)(4) While the operator license is in effect, state/local permits, additional licenses, certifications or authorizations are not required to exercise its rights to the extent obtaining or maintaining them would in practice halt or substantially delay use of the easement. This paragraph expressly preserves federal law, permits, authorizations and agency powers.
  • (e)(3) State-court orders, injunctions and other relief in proceedings where the United States is not a party cannot prevent the Defense Secretary exercising this authority or the operator using acquired easements. This is not a claim that every court order disappears.
  • (d)(2)(B) Separately, no California or other state-court order, judgment or determination can be a basis for revoking, suspending, modifying or terminating the issued operator license. This branch does not contain the (e)(3) US-not-a-party limitation.
  • (e)(1) Preempts state requirements that prevent, condition or delay the Defense Secretary exercising this section’s authority, or prevent the operator using easements acquired under it. This is not an exemption from all laws.
