### H.R.30 sex-offense and domestic-violence immigration grounds

**Compact:** Foushee opposed passage of H.R.30 with those provisions; no preferred alternative is established. H.R.30 would add sex-offense and specified domestic-violence-related inadmissibility grounds, add conviction-based sex-offense deportability, and broaden the existing domestic-violence deportability definition.

**Detail:**

**house:119:1:17**

Foushee opposed passage of H.R.30 with those provisions; no preferred alternative is established.

The exact House-passed H.R.30 adds INA 212(a)(2)(J) inadmissibility for a person convicted of, admitting commission of, or admitting acts constituting the essential elements of a sex offense under 34 USC 20911(5), or a conspiracy to commit one. New 212(a)(2)(K) uses those same conviction/admission predicates for a crime of domestic violence (defined through 237(a)(2)(E)), stalking, child abuse/neglect/abandonment, or a crime violating the protective-order portion addressing credible threats, repeated harassment or bodily injury. The INA baseline makes these inadmissibility grounds relevant to visas and admission; the bill is not limited by its title to persons unlawfully present. Noncriminal behavior mentioned in the victim-services portion of that definition is not itself converted into a crime by this bill. The incorporated sex-offense definition covers criminal sexual-act/contact offenses, specified offenses against minors, listed federal/military offenses, and attempts/conspiracies, with its foreign-conviction fairness and consensual-conduct limitations. The latter exclude specified consensual adult conduct unless custodial authority exists, and specified conduct where the victim is at least 13 and the offender is not more than 4 years older. Some covered conduct already can trigger existing criminal inadmissibility or deportability grounds; these are additional grounds and a definition expansion, not the creation of all immigration consequences for these crimes. INA 101(a)(48) supplies the existing conviction framework, including specified withheld adjudications with a guilt/plea/facts predicate plus court-ordered punishment, penalty or restraint. H.R.30 references 20911(5), rather than expressly importing the separate 20911(8) juvenile-conviction definition. The bill leaves the 237(a)(7) discretionary battered-person waiver for specified domestic-violence/stalking/protective-order deportability grounds intact; that waiver is not written to cover the new sex-offense ground. Existing 212(d)(3) discretionary temporary nonimmigrant admission and 212(d)(13)/(14) T/U status waiver authorities remain within their own conditions. ### H.R.35 immigration consequences for specified border vehicle flight

**Compact:** Foushee opposed passage of H.R.35 with those provisions; no preferred alternative is established. H.R.35 would create a specified intentional vehicle-flight offense within 100 miles of the U.S. border and add conviction/admission-based inadmissibility, deportability and an immigration-relief bar including asylum.

**Detail:**

**house:119:1:42**

Foushee opposed passage of H.R.35 with those provisions; no preferred alternative is established.

The exact House-passed H.R.35 adds 18 USC 40B: operating a motor vehicle within 100 miles of the United States border while intentionally fleeing a pursuing U.S. Border Patrol agent acting under lawful authority, or a pursuing Federal/State/local officer actively assisting or under Border Patrol command. The ordinary penalty alternatives are imprisonment up to 2 years, a title 18 fine, or both; where serious bodily injury results the imprisonment alternative is 5–20 years, and where any person's death results it is 10 years to life, with fine-or-both alternatives retained in both cases. The bill adds INA 212(a)(2)(J) inadmissibility and 237(a)(2)(G) deportability for any alien convicted of, admitting commission of, or admitting acts constituting the essential elements of 40B(a). It also inserts 208A making a person meeting that same predicate ineligible for relief under the immigration laws, expressly including asylum under 208. Neither arrest alone nor merely being within 100 miles is the printed immigration predicate. Pre-existing 18 USC 758 already criminalizes vehicle flight/evasion from an immigration or other federal checkpoint followed by fleeing law-enforcement agents above the legal speed limit, with up-to 5 years/fine/both; existing 237(a)(2)(A)(iv) already makes a conviction for 758 deportable. H.R.35 creates a different officer/geography/intent predicate and additional immigration consequences, rather than the first-ever immigration checkpoint-flight offense or deportability ground. Unlike that existing deportability ground, the newG also accepts specified admissions. Existing INA 101(a)(48) conviction rules remain. The Attorney General, with the Secretary of Homeland Security, must report annually to both Judiciary Committees on violations, charged/apprehended/not-charged/not-apprehended counts, and penalties sought/imposed. A reporting duty is not evidence of actual violations, convictions or enforcement. The separate broad relief-bar clause is preserved as printed; it is not replaced by a blanket claim that existing narrow waivers guarantee relief, and no individual asylum or removal outcome is decided.

- The imprisonment ranges retain fine-or-both alternatives in the printed clauses. Serious bodily injury or death must result from commission of the offense for the corresponding enhanced alternative; actual harm or guilt is not established here.
- New inadmissibility, deportability and relief ineligibility require conviction or the specified admissions of commission/essential acts. They do not follow merely from an arrest. The 208A relief bar expressly includes asylum; the candidate preserves its breadth without deciding individual relief/waiver interactions or actual deportation.
- Existing high-speed checkpoint-flight law and its conviction-based deportability are retained, including the old 237(a)(2)(A)(vi) pardon exception for that ground. That enumerated exception is not automatically applied to newG. H.R.35 adds distinct grounds; newJ/G labels are evaluated within this exact proposal, rather than combined with same-numbered grounds in parallel pending bills.

**Compact:** Foushee supported suspending the rules and passing H.R.993 with its border-technology planning, innovation-team authority and procedures/reporting. **Detail:**

Foushee supported suspending the rules and passing H.R.993 with its border-technology planning, innovation-team authority and procedures/reporting.

The exact H.R.993 EH proposal requires the DHS Secretary, acting through the CBP Commissioner and DHS Science and Technology Under Secretary, to submit a plan within 180 days of enactment to the House Homeland Security and Senate Homeland Security and Governmental Affairs Committees. The plan is to identify, integrate and deploy emerging/advanced technologies to enhance or address gaps in border-security operations. Its prescribed contents cover innovation-team use and composition, contributions and coordination, technologies from other agencies, procurement authorities and need for alternatives, scaling programs, program goals/timelines, border-community privacy/security impacts, replacement costs, research/private-sector coordination, metrics and listed aircraft/surveillance/inspection/tunnel/communications technologies.
